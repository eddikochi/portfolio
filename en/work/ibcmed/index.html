Digital governance · Platform migration · Go-live

FROM CHAOS
TO SYSTEM.

The bridge between marketing, product and technology at the most critical moment of an institutional website: migrating from WordPress to AEM, mid-deploy for go-live, with no integration between teams and no defined process.

RoleBridge: product / marketing / tech
ContextWordPress → AEM · go-live
SectorMedical postgraduate education
StackAEM · Monday · Figma · Miro

Context

A platform migration at the worst possible time.

The client was a large medical postgraduate institution, with campuses in several capitals and an extensive catalog of specializations. The institutional website, a central piece of enrollment acquisition, was being migrated from WordPress to Adobe Experience Manager (AEM).

I joined right at the go-live deploy — the moment when the most things can go wrong and the fewest people have visibility of the whole picture. The migration was moving forward, but without the structure to sustain it.

Problem

Migration without governance, teams without a bridge.

The technical project existed, but it was missing the layer that connects people, process and platform.

  • Marketing, product and tech in silos, with no integration.
  • No defined UX flow between interface and component in AEM.
  • Three environments (dev, stage, prod) with no clear promotion and validation.
  • Confusing backlog, hard for implementers to interpret.
  • No post-go-live QA: bugs only surfaced in production.
Core insightBeing the bridge between teams is less about relaying conversations and more about designing the processes that make the conversation unnecessary.

My role

Being the bridge and building the missing rails.

  • Segmented and clustered the disorganized operation by topic.
  • Integrated the teams around a shared demand and decision flow.
  • Redesigned the content implementation process with the devs.
  • Created a process map for the product → dev handoff.
  • Optimized frontend UX and implemented content on the site.
  • Built a QA flow and cleared the bug backlog.
  • Implemented A/B testing where there was no experimentation culture.

Constraints

What limited the work.

  • Timing. I joined mid-go-live — operating and improving at the same time.
  • New platform. AEM had just replaced WordPress, with flows not yet adapted.
  • Entrenched silos. Alignment wasn't technical — it was process and translation.
  • Content scale. Many course pages across multiple campuses multiplied any issue.

Decisions

Two redesigns that unblocked the operation.

Product → dev handoff map

A clear path from idea to a dev-ready task — less rework and predictable delivery.

Process optimization

Inserting a photo took 3–4 steps. I redesigned the flows with the devs, removing steps kept only by inertia.

Clustered backlog

Chaotic queue reorganized by topic, with readable stories.

QA flow from scratch

Validation before and after production, clearing the bug backlog.

Deliverables

What came out of it, concretely.

  • Product → dev handoff map.
  • Shortened implementation processes.
  • Reorganized, readable backlog.
  • QA flow built from scratch.
  • A/B tests implemented.
  • Frontend UX adjusted on course pages.

Impact

From migration chaos to an operation that runs on its own.

The impact was operational and qualitative — the kind of change that doesn't show up in a single metric, but that the whole team feels.

Before

Teams in silos · confusing handoff · 3–4 step processes · bugs discovered by chance in production.

After

Teams integrated by a shared flow · clear handoff · shortened processes · QA catches bugs before launch.

About the results: the gain here is operational and qualitative. I don't stand behind conversion numbers — I stand behind the governance structure, handoff and QA that now exist.

Learning

The most valuable work isn't always the most visible.

No one sees a "handoff map" or a "shortened process" from the outside, but those invisible structures decide whether a digital operation works or gets stuck. Being the bridge between teams is less about relaying conversations and more about designing the processes that make the conversation unnecessary.
