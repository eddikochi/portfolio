From migration to go-live: governance of a medical graduate-studies site

I was brought in to be the bridge between marketing, product and technology at the most critical moment of an institutional site: the migration from WordPress to AEM, mid deploy to go-live, with no integration between teams and no defined process.

02 — Context

A platform migration at the worst possible moment

The client was a large medical graduate-studies institution, with campuses in several capitals and an extensive catalog of specializations for physicians. The institutional site, the core of enrollment, was being migrated from WordPress to Adobe Experience Manager (AEM).

I came in exactly at the deploy-to-go-live moment: the point when the most can go wrong and the fewest people have a view of the whole. The migration was moving, but without the structure to sustain it. My job was to turn the chaos into a governable operation.

03 — Problem

Migration without governance, teams without a bridge

The technical project existed, but the layer that connects people, process and platform was missing. The symptoms:

  • No integration between teams. Marketing, product and tech in silos.
  • No UX-to-implementation flow. No defined path between interface and component in AEM.
  • Three environments, no process. Dev, stage and prod with no clear promotion and validation.
  • Confusing backlog. Stories hard to understand for whoever would implement them.
  • No post-go-live QA. Bugs only surfaced when someone bumped into them in production.
Core insight

Being the bridge between areas is less about mediating conversations and more about designing the processes that make the conversation unnecessary.

04 — My role

Be the bridge and build the missing rails

I came in as the link between marketing, product and technology. Being the “bridge” wasn’t just mediating conversations: it was designing the processes that let these areas work together without friction. In practice, I:

  • took the disorganized operation and segmented and clustered it by topic;
  • integrated the teams around a common demand-and-decision flow;
  • redesigned the content implementation process together with the devs;
  • created a process map for the product → dev handoff;
  • optimized the frontend UX and implemented content on the site;
  • created a QA flow and cleared the bug backlog to zero;
  • implemented A/B tests where there was no experimentation culture.
05 — Constraints

What limited the work

  • Timing. I came in mid go-live, with the operation having to run and improve at the same time.
  • New platform. AEM had just replaced WordPress, with flows not yet adapted.
  • Entrenched silos. Aligning wasn’t technical, it was process and translation.
  • Content scale. Many course pages across several campuses multiplied any problem.

07 — Key decisions

Two redesigns that unblocked the operation

The product → dev handoff map

The biggest bottleneck wasn’t technical: it was communication. Stories arrived hard to interpret, and the dev lost time decoding the request. I created a process map for the handoff: a clear path for how a demand goes from idea to dev, ready to implement. The result was less rework, less noise and more predictable delivery.

Optimizing the implementation processes

Some processes were absurdly long. The example that illustrates it best: inserting a simple photo on the site required a 3-to-4-step process. Multiplied by the volume of pages and campuses, it consumed enormous time. I redesigned these flows with the devs, shortening paths and cutting steps that only existed out of inertia.

10 — Impact

From migration chaos to an operation that runs on its own

The impact was operational and qualitative: the kind of change that doesn’t show up in a single metric, but that the whole team feels:

Before

  • Marketing, product and tech in silos.
  • Confusing handoff: the dev decoded the request.
  • 3-4 step processes for trivial tasks.
  • Bugs discovered by chance, in production.

After

  • Teams integrated by a common flow.
  • Clear handoff: the dev receives ready demands.
  • Shortened processes, freeing up time.
  • A QA flow catches bugs before they go live.
11 — Learnings

What stayed

The most valuable work often isn’t the most visible. No one sees a “handoff map” or a “shortened process” from the outside, but it’s these invisible structures that decide whether a digital operation works or stalls. Being the bridge between areas is less about mediating conversations and more about designing the processes that make the conversation unnecessary.
