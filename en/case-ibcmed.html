Digital governance · platform migration · go-live

From migration to go-live: governance of a medical postgraduate website

I was brought in to be the bridge between marketing, product and technology at the most critical moment of an institutional website: the migration from WordPress to AEM, in the middle of the go-live deploy, with no team integration and no defined process. My work was to turn chaos into a governable operation — and redesign the processes that were blocking delivery.

02 — Context

A platform migration at the worst possible moment

The client was a large-scale medical postgraduate institution, with locations in several Brazilian state capitals and an extensive catalog of specialization courses for physicians. The institutional website — the central piece of lead capture — was being migrated from WordPress to Adobe Experience Manager (AEM).

I was brought in exactly at the go-live deploy moment: when the most things can go wrong and the fewest people have a view of the whole picture. The migration was moving forward, but without the structure to sustain it.

03 — Problem

Migration without governance, teams without a bridge

The technical project existed, but the layer that connects people, process and platform was missing. The symptoms:

  • No team integration. Marketing, product and tech operated in silos, with no shared communication or decision flow.
  • No UX-to-implementation flow. There was no defined path between the designed interface and AEM component implementation.
  • Three environments, no process. AEM ran on dev, stage and prod, but without a clear promotion and validation process between them.
  • Confusing backlog. User stories were hard to interpret for implementers — the dev received requests without clarity on what to do.
  • No post-go-live QA. Bugs on many course pages only surfaced when someone stumbled on them in production.
  • No testing culture. There were no A/B tests or structured improvement hypotheses.
Central insight

Being the bridge between areas is less about mediating conversations and more about designing the processes that make those conversations unnecessary.

04 — My role

Being the bridge — and building the rails that were missing

I joined as the link between marketing, product and technology. But being a "bridge" wasn't just about mediating conversations: it was about designing the processes that allowed these areas to work together without friction. In practice, I:

  • took the disorganized operation and segmented and clustered it by topic, bringing order to what was arriving loose;
  • integrated the teams around a shared demand and decision flow;
  • redesigned the content implementation process together with the developers;
  • created a process map for the product → dev handoff;
  • optimized the frontend UX and implemented content on the site;
  • created a QA flow and cleared the accumulated bug backlog;
  • implemented A/B tests where no testing culture had existed before.
05 — Constraints

What limited the work

  • Timing. I joined mid go-live — there was no luxury of stopping everything to reorganize; the operation had to run and improve simultaneously.
  • New platform. AEM had just replaced WordPress, and workflows hadn't yet adapted to the new CMS.
  • Consolidated silos. Marketing, product and tech had different languages and priorities; alignment was not a technical matter, but one of process and translation.
  • Content scale. Many course pages, spread across multiple locations, multiplied any process problem.
06 — Process

Diagnose → Structure → Document → Validate → Optimize

  1. DiagnosisMap what was coming in, from whom, and where it got stuck
  2. StructuringCluster demands by topic
  3. DocumentationHandoff map and readable backlog
  4. ValidationQA flow built from scratch
  5. OptimizationShortened processes and A/B tests

Diagnosis. The first task was to understand a "messy room": map what was coming in, from whom, in what state, and where it got stuck. Without that map, any solution would be a shot in the dark.

Structuring. I separated and clustered demands by topic, turning a chaotic queue into manageable categories. This gave the teams a shared language to talk about the work.

Documentation. I created the product → dev handoff map and rewrote backlog stories so the developer understood what needed to be done — without rounds of "what did you mean by this?".

Validation. I built a QA flow that didn't exist, validating pages before and after they went to production, and clearing the accumulated bug backlog.

Optimization. With the operation stable, I tackled the slow processes and implemented A/B tests to start making decisions based on behavior, not opinion.

07 — Key decisions

Two redesigns that unblocked the operation

Of everything I optimized, two processes summarize the work's impact:

The product → dev handoff map

The biggest bottleneck wasn't technical — it was communication. Backlog stories arrived hard to interpret, and the developer lost time deciphering the request before executing it. I created a process map for the handoff between product and development: a clear path for how a demand moves from idea to the dev ready for implementation. The result was less rework, less noise and more predictable deliveries.

Optimizing the implementation processes

Some processes were absurdly long for what they delivered. The clearest example: inserting a simple photo on the site required a 3–4 step process. Multiplied by the volume of course pages and locations, this consumed enormous operational time. I redesigned these flows together with the developers, shortening paths and eliminating steps that only existed through inertia. Tasks that previously bottlenecked the team became trivial.

10 — Impact

From migration chaos to a self-running operation

The impact was operational and qualitative — the kind of change that doesn't show up in a single metric, but that everyone on the team feels:

Before

  • Marketing, product and tech operating in silos.
  • Confusing handoff — the dev decoded the request before executing it.
  • 3–4 step processes for trivial tasks.
  • Bugs discovered by accident, already in production.
  • Decision driven by opinion.

After

  • Teams integrated by a shared flow and language.
  • Clear handoff — the dev receives ready demands, with less rework.
  • Shortened processes, freeing up operational time.
  • QA flow catches bugs before they go live.
  • Decision based on data, via A/B tests.
11 — Learnings

What stayed

The most valuable work is often the least visible. Nobody sees a "handoff map" or a "shortened implementation process" from the outside — but those are exactly the invisible structures that decide whether a digital operation works or stalls. I learned that being the bridge between areas is less about mediating conversations and more about designing the processes that make those conversations unnecessary.

12 — Next steps

Possible evolution

  • Turn the handoff map and QA flow into a replicable Digital Governance Kit — an asset that applies the same method to other operations.
  • Evolve the A/B testing culture into a structured CRO program, with hypotheses and prioritization.
